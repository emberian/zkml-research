      Abstract.     We explicitly construct and benchmark the first lattice-based IVC
      scheme from folding. The scheme supports customizable constraint systems over
      rings which we exploit to obtain proofs of correct execution of an FHE bootstrapping,
      a critical component of verifiable FHE. Notably and of independent interest, we
      introduce a novel CCS relation capable of performing automorphism stability checks
      which yields better expressivity for CCS over rings. We use this new relation to
      arithmetize the folding scheme verifier as well as TFHE’s bootstrapping operation, and
      measure the performance of our folding scheme implementation on this arithmetization.
      Benchmarks indicate smaller proofs compared to the state of the art at the cost
      of a sharp increase in prover and verifier time. Lastly, we consider the security of
      folding-based IVC schemes with a super-constant number of recursive rounds and
      give an argument for the knowledge soundness of our construction in the ROM. Our
      work also discusses and highlights key open questions for future work, such as the
      design of hash functions over rings that permit efficient arithmetizations.
      Keywords: Verifiable FHE · IVC · Folding · TFHE.


1     Introduction
Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) is a cryptographic primitive which allows one
to perform arbitrary computations over encrypted data. It has gained considerable
traction in applications where computation on sensitive data is outsourced to a third
party. Unfortunately, protocols based on FHE in its most fundamental form are only
known to be secure in the semi-honest model where interacting parties are assumed to
follow a prescribed protocol perfectly. In particular, FHE provides no guarantee that
the third party carries out the intended outsourced computation. As such, several recent
works [GNS23, KVMH24, MN24, TW25, Wal24, LLZ+ 25] have begun studying the security
and performance of homomorphic encryption in the fully malicious model (where parties
may deviate from the protocol). These works follow the same approach: the security
guarantees for the fully malicious setting are obtained by taking a semi-honest secure FHE
scheme and using verifiable computation (VC) to force potentially malicious parties to
behave honestly. This combination of FHE and VC is referred to as verifiable FHE (VFHE).
It is a crucial requirement that a VFHE scheme is efficiently verifiable. In particular,
verification ought to be much more efficient than carrying out the FHE evaluation itself.
To date, concrete VFHE proposals remain relatively impractical, particularly in terms of
proving time e.g. [TW25] or verification time and proof size e.g. [LLZ+ 25].
    This work follows the same blueprint by focusing on providing integrity guarantees for
fully homomorphic computation. Similarly to [TW25, LLZ+ 25], we make the observation
that the FHE bootstrapping operation which manages ciphertext “noise” is the most

crucial and computationally expensive part of any FHE scheme. As such, we propose
a novel method to prove correctness of (the most significant part of) the bootstrapping
operation from TFHE [CGGI20], a practical and well-known FHE scheme with a relatively
lightweight bootstrapping operation. We also leverage incrementally verifiable computation
(IVC) [Val08] in conjunction with the structure of TFHE’s bootstrapping to prove its
correct execution. Our work separates itself from prior art in that it exploits the folding-
based approach of building IVC rather than the recursive succinct non-interactive argument
of knowledge (SNARK) technique. This newer approach is introduced in [BGH19] and
promises better performances than the SNARK-based approach introduced in [Val08]
due to relatively small recursive verifier circuits. Since the introduction of folding, a
flurry of works have emerged refining this technique [BCMS20, BCL+ 21, BDFG21, KST22,
KS24, BC23, RZ23]. In particular, LatticeFold [BC24] distinguishes itself from the rest by
introducing a folding scheme based on lattices (rather than elliptic curves) for customizable
constraint system (CCS) [STW23] relations over rings (rather than fields). This is of
particular interest for VFHE as modern FHE schemes make extensive use of ring arithmetic
for performance purposes. In fact, obtaining VFHE by expressing ring arithmetic using
CCS over fields and applying a SNARK for CCS incurs significant overhead as observed
by [KVMH24, TW25, LLZ+ 25], leaving room for potentially vast improvements if CCS
over rings were to be exploited. This overhead is naturally explained by observing that a
ring multiplication may be expressed as an arithmetic circuit over a field using a number
of gates linearithmic in the ring degree whereas in the ring regime, the same multiplication
may be expressed with a single gate. The aforementioned prior works leave open the
task of studying VFHE in the promising context of CCS over rings, which we tackle in
this work by building LatticeFold-based IVC and applying it to TFHE’s bootstrapping
operation. Along the way, we identify and fix problems with applying existing ring-based
folding schemes described in the literature.

1.1    Contributions
We set out to construct the IVC scheme from lattice-based folding for CCS relations
over rings and apply it to an FHE scheme in order to obtain VFHE. More precisely, we
remark that the bootstrapping operation is the costliest operation of any FHE scheme
so we constrain the scope of our efforts to applying our new IVC construction to the
bootstrapping of TFHE [CGGI20] which is relatively lightweight compared to other FHE
schemes.
    To construct the new IVC scheme for CCS over rings, we start from the prior work
LatticeFold [BC24] which builds the first folding scheme based on the plausibly post-
quantum secure Module Short Integer Solution (MSIS) problem [LM06, PR06]. As
observed in Nova [KST22], folding schemes for CCS are sufficient to construct IVC: it
suffices to express the folding scheme verifier as a CCS relation and recursively fold this
relation into an accumulator relation. While folding-based IVC has been achieved in prior
works in the field-CCS regime, this work is to the best of our knowledge, the first effort
towards explicitly translating this technique to the ring-CCS regime.
    Looking ahead, in Section 3 we observe that CCS over rings is not expressive enough to
constrain computations of interest. We thus introduce a novel CCS relation augmented with
automorphism checks in order to provide better expressivity and permit the construction
of IVC for circuits over rings. The introduction of automorphisms allows for better
manipulation of individual coefficients of ring elements, or ring elements that ought to
be constants. As an example, one might wish to show that a particular entry of a CCS
witness is a constant monomial. In the usual case of cyclotomic rings modulo a prime
q, these constant checks can be performed by arguing that the witness entry is fixed
under just two automorphisms [dPLS18]. As the LatticeFold verification circuit depends
on equations where certain elements are constant (in particular equations over Zq ), the
Amit Deo, Louis Tremblay Thibault                                                             3


introduction of automorphisms becomes extremely useful for building IVC. In addition,
the automorphisms may independently be useful for expressing the computational step
that the IVC intends to prove. For further discussion including less effective approaches,
see Section 3.
     Complementarily, we also amend the LatticeFold folding scheme to support automor-
phism checks and we offer an integral proof of security of the amended protocol. Our
protocol follows the same recipe as the original LatticeFold protocol, but batches extra
terms into the sumcheck and linearization step that depends on automorphisms of the
witness. We also provide an open-source implementation1 of the augmented folding scheme
based on [Net] and measure its performance in the context of IVC using secure parameters
both for the folding scheme and TFHE. Using our implementation, we find that our
augmentation of LatticeFold only adds around a 12% overhead in verifier/prover time.
For this, we note that TFHE bootstrapping naturally consists of four sub-operations:
sample extraction, key switching, modulus switching and blind rotation. By far, the most
expensive operation in terms of bootstrapping time and proving time is the blind rotation.
Further, blind rotation repeats a computation step n times where n ∼ 210 . It is estimated
in [TW25, Sec. 4] that the cost of proving key switching and modulus switching is the cost
of just one of these n steps. Moreover, [TW25, Sec. 4] shows that the sample extraction
may be integrated into the key switch. Assuming linearity in prover time, the non-blind
rotation steps of the bootstrapping can be proven in a matter of a second or so. Folding
can then be used to do the bulk of the work for blind rotation.
     Overall, we estimate prover times of around 24 minutes per folding step. By incorporat-
ing 34 blind rotations per folding step, this results in overall proving times one to two orders
of magnitude slower and verifier times around 6× slower than the recursive Plonk-based
approach of [TW25]. For more details, see Section 5.4.On the positive side, the proof sizes
are around twice as small at around 85 kB. Our approach is further magnitudes slower in
prover time than the recent work [LLZ+ 25] that proposes a proof system with proof sizes
in the hundreds of megabytes. A major contributor to proving time/memory requirements
is the use of the Poseidon hash function as a random oracle over the field Zq [GKR+ 21].
Although Poseidon over Zq admits compact CCS arithmetization over Zq , it becomes rather
inefficient when arithmetizing over the cyclotomic rings used in lattice-based cryptography.
This is because a ring element must first be decomposed into multiple Zq elements before
it is fed into Poseidon. Further, the subsequent arithmetization of this entire process over
rings would interpret each Zq element as a ring element, wasting a lot of encoding space.
Unfortunately, we show that redefining Poseidon over cyclotomic rings in an attempt to
avoid this issue does not lead to random oracle-like behavior (see Section D for further
discussion). Therefore, we propose the open question of designing a hash function over
rings (in particular, power-of-two cyclotomic rings used in lattice-based cryptography)
that behaves like a random oracle and admits compact arithmetization over the ring. This
would significantly increase the efficiency of lattice-based folding approaches over rings.
     Lastly and of independent interest, we study the case of knowledge soundness of IVC
protocols with a super-constant number of recursive steps. This is crucial in our case
where the number of IVC steps is polynomial in the security parameter. Inspired by
the discussion on this subject from [GKO+ 23, Kot24], we present an argument for the
knowledge soundness of our IVC protocol in the Random Oracle Model (ROM) based on
the SIS assumption [Ajt96]. In short, we prove that an existing SIS-based PCS [CMNW24]
has computationally injective proofs and use the transformation of [Kot24] to construct
a straight-line extractable folding scheme. The straight-line property implies that no
rewinding is necessary for the folding scheme’s extractor which in turn yields a sound IVC
scheme with a polynomial number of steps. All in all, the resulting IVC instantiation relies
solely on lattice assumptions and is plausibly post-quantum secure.

1.2     Related works
The line of work of [FGP14, FNP20, BCFK21, GNS23, KVMH24] initiates the study of
verifiable homomorphic encryption but restricts itself to proving the correct execution of
leveled homomorphic schemes rather than fully homomorphic schemes. In other words,
these prior works do not attempt to prove the integrity of the bootstrapping operation
due to its sheer complexity.
    The prior work closest to ours is that of [TW25] which uses SNARK-based IVC to
obtain a proof of correct execution of TFHE’s bootstrapping. Our work separates itself
from [TW25] in two ways. First, we consider IVC built from folding rather than from
SNARKs, which promises better performances due to the fact that folding schemes are
weaker, simpler and more efficient than SNARKs [KST22]. Second, our architecture allows
to natively prove statements about ring arithmetic whereas [TW25] suffers from some
overhead due to the need to emulate ring arithmetic using an arithmetic circuit over a
field.
    HasteBoots [LLZ+ 25] also tackles the problem of proving the correct execution of
TFHE’s bootstrapping, offering a trade-off with faster prover time but bigger proofs
compared to [TW25].
    On the folding side, LatticeFold+ [BC25] and Neo [NS25] have emerged concurrently
to our work. The former, being a follow-up work to LatticeFold [BC24], is expected to
yield performance improvements for our architecture. However, it falls out of scope of this
contribution and we leave its integration and implementation as future work. On the other
hand, Neo is a new lattice-based folding scheme but targets CCS over fields. Since our
goal involves proving the correct execution of ring arithmetic, this folding scheme is not
a particularly promising candidate compared to other folding schemes based on elliptic
curves.

1.3     Paper organization
We start in Section 2 by giving some background and recalling useful notions. In Section 3
we justify and introduce our novel CCS with automorphisms over rings relation, and
amend the batched protocol introduced in LatticeFold [BC24]. We discuss in Section 4
the knowledge soundness of our folding scheme where the number of recursive steps is
super-constant. In Section 5 we use our new folding scheme to construct an IVC protocol.
We also give an arithmetization of TFHE’s blind rotation operation (by far the costliest
step of bootstrapping) for CCS with automorphisms over rings. Combining with SNARKs
for the remaining steps (i.e., modulus switching/key switching/sample extraction) allows to
prove correct execution of the entire bootstrapping operation. Lastly, we give performance
measures of our prototype and provide comparisons with prior work.

2.4      TFHE
We start by giving a quick overview of the TFHE scheme [CGGI20] whose bootstrapping
operation is the focus of our work. The aim is to give an intuitive understanding of the
scheme without overloading the reader’s attention with formalities. For a more precise
description, we refer to the original work [CGGI20] and a follow-up systematization of
knowledge [Joy22].
    TFHE is an FHE scheme based on the (Ring) Learning With Errors ((R)LWE)
problem [Reg05, LPR10]. As with all existing FHE schemes, ciphertexts have noise levels
associated to them. It is important that this noise does not increase beyond some fixed
threshold, otherwise the decryption algorithm fails. In order to allow for an unbounded
amount of homomorphic computation, a bootstrapping operation is used to reset the noise
of a ciphertext down to some predetermined level. Several ciphertext types, which are
described in what follows, are used in the TFHE bootstrapping operation.

2.4.2   Programmable bootstrapping

Definition 6 (Reduction of Knowledge [KP23]). Consider ternary NP relations R1 and R2
comprised of public parameters, instances inst and witnesses wit. A reduction of knowledge
(RoK) Π : R1 → R2 is a triple of PPT algorithms (Setup, Prove, Vf) such that

   • Setup(1n ) → pp: takes as input a security parameter and returns public parameters.

   • Prove(pp, inst1 , wit1 ) → (inst2 , wit2 ): given a statement (pp, inst1 , wit1 ) ∈ R1 , Prove
     reduces it interactively to a second statement (pp, inst2 , wit2 ) ∈ R2 .

   • Vf(pp, inst1 ) → inst2 : given an instance inst1 corresponding to R1 , Vf reduces
     interactively the task of verifying inst1 to that of verifying inst2 corresponding to R2 .

Let hProve(pp, inst1 , wit1 ), Vf(pp, inst1 )i 7→ (inst2 , wit2 ) denote the interaction transcript
between the verifier and the prover. A reduction of knowledge satisfies the following
properties.

   1. Completeness: for any PPT adversary A, given pp ← Setup(1n ) and (inst1 , wit1 ) ←
      A(pp) such that (pp, inst1 , wit1 ) ∈ R1 , it must be the case that
      (pp, hProve(pp, inst1 , wit1 ), Vf(pp, inst1 )i) ∈ R2 .

   2. Knowledge soundness: for any expected polynomial time (EPT) adversary
      (A, Prove∗ ), there exists an EPT extractor E such that given pp ← Setup(1n ) and
      (inst1 , st) ← A(pp), we have

               | Pr[(pp, inst1 , E(pp, inst1 , st) ∈ R1 )]
                      − Pr[(pp, hProve∗ (pp, inst1 , st), Vf(pp, inst1 )i) ∈ R2 ]| ∈ negl(n).

      where st denotes the internal state of the adversary and contains the random coins
      used by Prove∗ .

   3. Public reducibility: there exists a polynomial function f such that for any PPT
      adversary A and EPT adversary Prove∗ , given pp ← Setup(1n ), (inst1 , st) ← A(pp)
      and (inst2 , wit2 ) ← hProve∗ (pp, inst1 , st), Vf(pp, inst1 )i with interaction transcript tr,
      we have that f (pp, inst1 , tr) 7→ inst2 .

A reduction of knowledge is said to be public-coin if the verifier’s messages are solely
comprised of random coins. A public-coin reduction of knowledge can be made non-
interactive using the Fiat-Shamir heuristic [FS87].

3       Augmenting CCS over Rings
Motivation In the argument literature, arithmetization techniques [GGPR13, STW23]
are typically adapted to arithmetic circuits over finite fields. Enforcing witness constraints
using circuit structure and public input/output is generally straightforward and well-
defined in this regime. However, when working in the more general setting of arithmetic
circuits over rings, things become a bit more subtle. Consider for example a prime q
and the ring Rq := Zq [X]/hX d + 1i. There exists a natural mapping f from a circuit C
over Fq to the same circuit over Rq that embeds Fq -elements in Rq -elements as constant
monomials. More precisely, f maps a ∈ Fq to a + 0 · X + 0 · X 2 + . . . + 0 · X d−1 ∈ Rq . As
such, the language of arithmetic circuits over Rq is NP-complete: it is efficiently verifiable
given a witness and f reduces the NP-hard language of arithmetic circuits over fields to it.
In contrast however, not all circuits satisfiable over rings are satisfiable over fields. For
example, given a ring R which is not an integral domain and a, b, c ∈ R with a being
a divisor of 0, we may have that ab = ac but b 6= c. In other words, even for simple
constraints unsatisfiable over Fq there might exist several satisfying witnesses over Rq ,
indicating a slack in the expressivity of circuits over rings compared to circuits over fields.
We now discuss the LatticeFold verification circuit represented as CCS over Rq which
could be used to build IVC. Part of the LatticeFold verification procedure checks that

LatticeFold transcript satisfy some relation over Zq . As a concrete example, imagine that

4     Straight-Line extractability
We briefly recall a discussion from [Kot24, Section 7]. As observed in this prior work,
sequentially composing a pair of reductions of knowledge Π1 and Π2 yields a reduction
of knowledge Π := Π1 ◦ Π2 for which the extractor EΠ runs the extractors EΠ2 and EΠ1
in sequence. This becomes problematic when the extractors devised for the respective
reductions of knowledge need a polynomially sized tree of accepting transcripts: each leaf
of this tree must be extracted using another extractor which needs another polynomially
sized tree of accepting transcripts and so forth. In other words, the composed extractor
has runtime exponential in the number of compositions.
     This does not pose a problem if the number of compositions is assumed to be a fixed
constant, as proposed in [KST22]. However, in our context, the number of compositions
is linear in the length of the TFHE bootstrapping key, which is itself polynomial in the
security parameter n. This is catastrophic as the knowledge soundness proof crucially relies
on the fact that the extractor runs in expected polynomial time in n. If the composed
extractor takes exponential time to extract a witness, then the knowledge soundness proof
does not hold.
     There is however a stronger notion of knowledge soundness that allows us to bypass
this issue in the ROM: straight-line extractability. A reduction of knowledge is said to be
straight-line extractable in the ROM if an expected polynomial time extractor can extract
a witness from a single accepting transcript and given access to the list of random oracle
queries and responses. Sequential composition of straight-line extractable reductions of
knowledge incurs only additive overhead to the composed extractor, allowing us to let the
number of compositions be polynomial in the security parameter.
     In what follows, we show how the composed reduction of knowledge of interest in-
troduced in Section 3.1.1 can be transformed into a straight-line extractable reduction
of knowledge. [Kot24] provides two helpful building blocks, the first of which takes a
polynomial commitment scheme with computationally injective proofs (Definition 14) and
transforms it into a straight-line extractable proof of opening knowledge. The second
transformation takes a straight-line extractable proof of opening knowledge and a reduction
of knowledge which contains a commitment to the witness in the instance and transforms
it into a straight-line extractable reduction of knowledge, which is our goal.
     We start by showing that the SIS-based polynomial commitment scheme introduced
in [CMNW24] satisfies computational proof injectivity without introducing any further
assumption. Using this result together with the two aforementioned transformations, we
conclude that (a small variation of) our reduction of knowledge presented in this work is
straight-line extractable.

4.2    Straight-Line extractable reduction of knowledge
Equipped with a polynomial commitment scheme PCS with computationally injective
proofs, we recall the strategy from [Kot24] to construct straight-line extractable proofs
of opening knowledge for PCS. Using this strategy as a building block, one can then
transform any RoK (where the input relation contains a PCS commitment to the witness)
into a straight-line extractable non-interactive RoK in the ROM [Kot24]. In more detail,
suppose we have a RoK Π : R1 → R2 where

       R1 := {((ppPCS , ppR ), (x, w̄), w)|(ppR , x, w) ∈ R ∧ w̄ = PCS.Com(ppPCS , w)}.   (6)

for some NP relation R. We devise a straight-line extractable RoK Π0 : R1 → R2 , which
proceeds as follows. The prover knows ((x, w̄), w) and the verifier knows (x, w̄). The prover
samples some evaluation point x from the domain of w (interpreted as a polynomial
function) and computes y ← w(x) and π ← PCS.Eval(pp, w̄, w, x, y). Since PCS satisfies
the evaluation binding property (Definition 5), we have that π attests to the fact that
w(x) = y. Then, the prover queries the random oracle on the tuple (x, x, y, π) to obtain
some random value ρ. The prover repeats this procedure until it obtains a response from
the random oracle such that ρ ≤ ν(n) for some ν ∈ negl(n). Then, the prover sends the
tuple (x, y, π) to the verifier, which verifies π according to PCS and checks that the random
oracle answers with some ρ ≤ ν(n) when queried on (x, x, y, π). If these conditions do
not hold, the verifier outputs ⊥ and terminates the protocol. Otherwise, the prover and
verifier proceed to the original protocol Π.

Claim. The tuple (x, y, π) is a straight-line extractable proof of opening knowledge in the
ROM.

Proof sketch. For brevity, we recall an informal argument from [Kot24, Section 7] to
support this claim and refer the reader to this work for details.
    Choosing a small enough function ν ∈ negl(n) ensures (with overwhelming probability)
that the prover must perform a number of random oracle queries which is greater than
deg(w). In this case, the extractor, having access to the random oracle queries (and
responses) from the prover, can use deg(w) + 1 different values of x and y included in each
ROM query to uniquely determine the witness polynomial w.
    Notice that a malicious PPT prover cannot flood the random oracle with queries
containing incoherent values of x and y as the proof of correct polynomial evaluation π
must be included in ROM queries and verified by the RoK verifier. Observe also that it
is difficult for a malicious PPT prover to generate two valid proofs π1 6= π2 in order to
obtain different random responses for the same evaluation point since the PCS satisfies
computational proof injectivity. This implies that if a prover is able to generate an
accepting proof of commitment opening knowledge, then with overwhelming probability
the extractor has enough information to extract the witness in the ROM.

    Note that by the binding property of the PCS, the witness w obtained from the straight-
line extractor must agree with the witness that would be extracted from the original non
straight-line RoK extractor, which allows us to conclude that the amended RoK is indeed
straight-line extractable in the ROM.

5     Folding-based IVC for the blind rotation
To construct IVC as proposed by Nova [KST22], one must express a non-interactive folding
scheme verifier in a representation suitable for the folding scheme, such as CCS or R1CS.
In our case, an ideal target representation would be CCS with automorphisms, but due to
a circular dependency in the CCS parameters of the folding verifier, we target R1CS with
automorphisms instead. Analogously to the way R1CS is a particular case of CCS, R1CS
with automorphisms is a particular case of CCS with automorphisms.
    Step 4 of the batched folding protocol induces a circular dependency on some CCS
parameters, which obliges us to fix these parameters a priori. Indeed, the first term
to compute as part of the verifier checks depends on the CCS parameters ns , ci and Si .
Naturally, the CCS representation of this step is a function of those parameters which
causes a self-referential issue. We overcome this hurdle by fixing the parameters to an
“R1CS with automorphisms” configuration, i.e. ns = 4, c1 = 1, c2 = c3 = c4 = −1, S1 =
{1, 2}, S2 = {3}, S3 = {4} and S4 = {5}. The CCS matrices M1 , M2 and M3 are set to the
R1CS matrices A, B and C respectively and M4 , M5 are set to the automorphism matrices
C 0 and C̃. An R1CS instance x is satisfied by a witness w if

5.1     Parameter Choices
We now overview parameter choices that we make for concreteness. The LatticeFold
implementation is written for relatively small ring dimension to optimize prover time
whereas TFHE uses a relatively large ring dimension (often 1024 or 2048) for correctness.
Therefore, we do not assume that the LatticeFold ring Rq and TFHE ring R̄q are the
same. Nonetheless, we show in Section B that TFHE ring operations can be reduced to
LatticeFold ring operations.

LatticeFold Parameters Typical TFHE implementations [Zam] use a modulus of
q = 264 . As a result, the most suitable ring amongst those implemented in [Net] is the
so-called “Frog Ring” with prime modulus q ≈ 264 and dimension 16. This ring splits into
fields of degree τ = 4. Furthermore, the LatticeFold decomposition parameters used in the
implementation are (b, k, B) = (2, 16, 216 ).

TFHE Parameters We set the TFHE modulus to equal the LatticeFold modulus chosen
above. The TFHE ring dimension we choose is 1024 allowing to use module rank k = 2.
The number of decomposition digits for TFHE bootstrapping is then set to ` = 1 and the
Amit Deo, Louis Tremblay Thibault                                                                   21


decomposition base is 232 . The LWE dimension (which is also the total number of blind
rotation steps) is 694.


Parameter optimization While the above set of parameters allow for a 128-bit secure
instantiation of the protocols described in this paper, optimizing parameter selection with
respect to the appropriate cost model is an entire endeavor in and of itself [BBB+ 23]. We
leave the optimization of LatticeFold with automorphisms and TFHE parameters in the

5.3    Batching Blind Rotations
For a single blind rotation, the constraints for the IVC circuit (including the blind rotation
system, the folding verifier system and various remaining hash checks) come out at around
222.1 (see Section C). As a result, we must run a folding step with 223 constraints/witnesses
in order to run the IVC despite the fact the IVC only requires folding for around 222.1
constraints. To reduce the number of IVC steps, we can batch n0 > 1 blind rotations into
a single IVC step without affecting performance as long as the IVC constraint system does
not spill over 223 . We next discuss how to pick n0 as large as possible.
    Firstly, let mBR and nBR be the number of R1CS constraints and witness elements
required for a single blind rotation step. Then the number of constraints required for
a batch of n0 blind rotation steps is mBR · n0 and the number of witness elements is
nBR · n0 − (k + 1) · (n0 − 1). For the latter, we note the output ciphertext for one blind
rotation is the input ciphertext for the next, so we can remove n0 − 1 repeated intermediate
witness ciphertexts per sequence of n0 blind rotations. Now, as n0 increases, the size of
the folding verifier increases very slowly since the verification complexity is logarithmic.
Additionally, the remaining parts of the IVC which are insignificant compared to folding
verification also remain insignificant for moderate values of n0 . As it turns out, setting n0
as large as 34 still keeps the overall IVC circuit under 223 .

Implementation In order to estimate the concrete performance of our IVC protocol, we
start with the implementation of LatticeFold provided by [Net]. This prototype implements
the less efficient unbatched sum-check version of the LatticeFold protocol. We thus set out
to implement the batched version of the protocol [BC24, Sec. 4.3]. We further augment
the prototype by adding support for the CCS with automorphisms over rings relation
(Definition 9) according to Section 3.1. With this implementation in hand, we are able to
measure the time taken to non-interactively fold relation instances that implement IVC.
    We give in Table 1 the performance measures of our prototype when folding CCS
instances with a number of constraints as previously discussed. Our benchmarks are taken
on an AWS HPC7a.96xlarge EC2 instance having 192 CPU cores and 768 GB memory.
Despite this brobdingnagian RAM capacity, we are unable to fold instances containing 222

Performance analysis Our IVC prototype built from lattice-based folding offers a
tradeoff compared to the current state of the art with comparable parameters [TW25,
LLZ+ 25]. More precisely, since the IVC requires 21 folding steps, we estimate that the
prover time is around 514 minutes whereas the verifier time is 49.07 ms. Therefore, our
prover and verifier time are respectively around 30× and 6× slower than that of [TW25].
However, our proofs are about twice as small. The tradeoff is exacerbated when comparing
to [LLZ+ 25], which proposes a scheme with a prover time orders of magnitude faster than
that of [TW25] at the cost of proof sizes in the hundreds of megabytes, which is impractical
for several applications such as blockchain-based protocols [Zam24, SDD+ 24].

C.3     Poseidon Challenges
We now overview the R1CS constraints associated with challenge generation using the
popular Poseidon [GKR+ 21] hash function over the field Zq . The parameters of the hash
function are labelled (RF , RP , α, r, c) and we will use the Frog ring Poseidon parameters
from the LatticeFold implementation (RF , RP , α, r, c) = (8, 22, 7, 20, 4) [Net].

Absorbs/Squeezes The Poseidon hash function uses the sponge construction. For rate
r, the sponge absorbs r elements of Zq at a time by adding them to the internal state and
applying the Poseidon permutation. The only non-linear aspect of the permutation is an
S-box that maps x ∈ Zq to xα ∈ Zq where α is some parameter typically chosen in {3, 5, 7}.
The total number of S-box applications in the permutation is RF · (r + c) + RP where c is
the capacity and RF , RP are the number of full and partial rounds respectively. Suppose
each S-box adds Sα R1CS constraints over Zq . For example, for α = 7, one can compute
x7 using square and multiply (i.e. computing from x, (x2 , x4 , x6 , x7 )) which adds S7 = 4
witness elements and constraints to the computation. Overall, a full absorb of r elements
in Zq costs Sα · (RF (r + c) + RP ) R1CS constraints and witness elements over Zq . We also
need to add in constant checking constraints. Note that we only need to check that the
absorbed elements are constant because the intermediate values contained in the witness are
guaranteed to be correct by the previously introduced constraints. Adding in the constant
checking constraints, a full absorb of r elements in Zq costs Sα ·(RF (r +c)+RP )+2r R1CS
constraints (with automorphisms) over Rq . When squeezing r elements of Zq , one does
not need to perform constant checks, meaning that only Sα · (RF (r + c) + RP ) constraints
are added.

is required to map τ elements of Zq into a ring element. For example, let ~c ∈ Zτq . Then

C.4     IVC Hashes
In order to build IVC for blind rotation, a couple of hash checks need to be introduced.
The first set of hash checks stems from the augmented function F 0 introduced in [KST22,
Fig. 3] introduced to compress instances. The second set of hash checks are introduced
to ensure that the blind rotation key components are all used in the correct order (see
[TW25, Sec. 3.2.1] for more details). We note that the hash randomness mentioned
in [KST22] is omitted here as we do not care about the hiding or ZK property. The three
hash checks/evaluations that are performed in the IVC compiler are then:

D         Ring-Based Poseidon
A great deal of inefficiency stems from the fact that ring elements must be translated to
Zq elements before being fed into Poseidon. One could overcome this issue by defining a
 10 this replaces a potentially larger vk
Amit Deo, Louis Tremblay Thibault                                                          53


hash function that takes as input arbitrary ring elements. Here, we discuss the use of a
ring-based Poseidon that absorbs/squeezes ring elements rather than Zq elements. We
recall that we use Poseidon for the Fiat-Shamir transform, so we require that Poseidon
behaves like a random oracle.
    Suppose for now that the ring splits fully into d copies of Zq via the NTT transformation.
We may then run Poseidon in parallel on each NTT slot. An important point here is
that Poseidon consists of only algebraic operations on its state, e.g. the S-box is described
by x 7→ xα where α is typically a small integer. Therefore, transferring this parallel
slot version of Poseidon back to the non-NTT ring domain yields a ring-based version of
Poseidon. In particular, the slot-wise operations x 7→ xα are mapped to ring operations
x 7→ xα . The linear operations in Poseidon transfer from their slot-wise versions in an
identical manner. Therefore, there is an equivalence between parallel slot-wise Poseidon
over the field Zq and ring-based Poseidon over a fully splitting ring. As such, under the
assumption that Poseidon over Zq behaves as a random oracle, ring-based Poseidon acts as
a random oracle independently in each individual slot. Unfortunately, these observations
also imply that ring-based Poseidon is easily distinguishable from a random oracle as the
distinct slots do not interact during hashing. For example, applying Poseidon to two ring
elements x1 and x2 that differ in just a single slot will yield two outputs y1 and y2 that
differ in that same slot.
    For LatticeFold rings that do not split fully, similar observations can be made using
NTT slots that are field extensions of Zq . A slight difference is that good cryptanalysis
of Poseidon only exists for fields of the form Zq , so we do not necessarily have random
oracle behaviour in individual slots. The study of Poseidon over extension fields is left for
now as an open problem11 . In any case, the same distinguishing attack that works on fully
splitting rings can be leveraged in the non-fully splitting case independently of whether
Poseidon over extension fields is secure.
    This state of affairs leads us to a seemingly important open problem: Is there a hash
function that (i) acts as a random oracle over Rq -inputs and (ii) permits an efficient
CCS/R1CS arithmetization over Rq ? Note that the requirements (i) and (ii) are somewhat
at odds. This is because efficient R1CS/CCS arithmetizations arise from heavy use of
algebraic operations, whereas our discussion of Poseidon suggests that using only algebraic
operations yields a trivial distinguishing attack. Therefore, a good solution to this problem
would likely make extensive use of algebraic operations interspersed with a relatively small
number of non-algebraic operations. The goal of the latter would be to mix the NTT
slots to break the parallel structure induced by the algebraic operations. We leave further
investigation to future work.
